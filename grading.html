Condition & grading.

How collectors talk about condition, what each grade means on the card and off it, what actually moves value on a Marvel Legends figure, how to photograph flaws honestly when you sell, and the short list of things you must never do to a figure. Part of the encyclopedia's grading overview.

The C-scale, C-10 down to C-1

The C-scale is collector shorthand, not an official standard: 10 is flawless, 1 is junk, and everything else lives between. The band column is the quick-read version you can use in a listing title. The same grade means different things depending on whether the figure is still on its card, a scuffed blister and a scuffed elbow are different problems, so read the right column.

The scale is a rule of thumb the hobby has used for decades across action-figure lines, it grades condition, not value. A C-10 common figure is still a common figure. Professional grading (AFA/CGA, CAS, UKG slabbed cases) exists for carded figures; as the encyclopedia notes, no Legends-specific grading premium has ever been documented, so pay for a slab only if you want the case itself.

MOC, MIB, and loose, the three states

MOC, Mint on Card

Unopened, on its original card, bubble intact and sealed. The grade describes the package: card, corners, bubble clarity. An MOC figure is valued for its packaging as much as its figure, which is why a C-8 MOC and a C-8 loose are priced worlds apart.

MIB, Mint in Box

In its original box. The tricky one: some sellers use MIB to mean "opened once, like new," others to mean "never removed." If you're selling, say which. If you're buying, ask, and treat an unanswered question as "opened."

Loose, out of the package

Opened and handled. Graded on the figure itself: paint, joints, completeness. Most played-with collections are C-6 to C-8 loose; that's normal, and honest C-7 descriptions sell faster than optimistic "mint" ones with hidden wear.

What actually affects value

Buyers pay for what they can see and verify. These are the defects that move a price, roughly in the order buyers care about them. Log them on the sold-price journal, grade before you log.

Carded: corners, edges, and bubble

Sharp corners and flat cards separate the top grades. On the bubble: clarity and yellowing (age-yellowing is normal on Toy Biz-era blisters and priced in), scuffs, cracks, and lifting where the blister meets the card. Price stickers and their residue are defects, removing a sticker cleanly is fine, but residue counts.

Loose: paint apps and joint tightness

Crisp, un-rubbed paint at the joints is the loose-figure equivalent of sharp corners, it's the first thing a buyer checks in photos. Tight joints that still hold a pose; "floppy" is a price drop, "glued" is a parts-bin figure. Stress marks (whitened plastic at joints) are wear and should be disclosed.

Completeness: accessories and BAF pieces

Every accessory that came in the box, plus the Build-A-Figure piece, a figure missing its BAF leg is an incomplete BAF, not a complete figure, and buyers price accordingly. Alternate hands, heads, and effect parts all count. Keep the tray insert and paperwork too; buyers of higher grades expect them.

Photographing flaws for listings

An honest photo set is the fastest trust signal a seller has. Bad photos cost you more than bad condition, buyers assume the worst about anything they can't see.

The light and the background

Daylight or a bright white lamp, neutral background. No flash hotspots on the bubble, angle the figure so the light doesn't wash out what you're trying to show. Phone cameras are fine; a steady hand beats a good lens.

The flaw shots (take all of these)

  • Carded: all four corners, the bubble straight-on and at an angle (shows scuffs and yellowing), back of card.
  • Loose: face/paint close-up, joint surfaces where rub happens, every accessory laid out in one frame.
  • One wide shot of the whole item, exactly as it ships.

Describe, don't sell

"C-7, corner wear top right, bubble lightly scuffed, see photo 3" beats "near mint!" every time. Name the band from the scale above and point at the photos that support it, buyers who trust your grading come back for your next listing.

Never do this

  • Never clean with solvents

    Rubbing alcohol, acetone, Goo Gone, bleach, they cloud bubbles, melt paint, and leave residue you can't undo. If it needs cleaning at all, a barely damp soft cloth is the ceiling.

  • Never try to "fix" sun-fading

    Sun-faded plastic and card can't be un-faded. Retr0bright-style treatments and repainting destroy more value than the fading did, and a touched-up figure sold without disclosure is a reputation problem waiting to happen.

  • Never reseal a bubble

    Re-gluing a lifted blister to pass a figure off as unopened is the fastest way to end up on a buyer's blocklist. An opened figure is a loose figure, price it honestly.

  • Never over-grade

    If the scale above says C-7, the listing says C-7, not "near mint." Generous grading produces returns, disputes, and one-star feedback that costs more than the grade bump ever earned.

Standard collector knowledge, kept deliberately general: nothing here is investment advice, and nothing here is a professional appraisal. For a genuinely rare or valuable piece, a professional grading service exists, but for most of a collection, honest self-grading with this scale is the whole game.
